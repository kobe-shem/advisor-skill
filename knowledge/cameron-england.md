# Cameron England — doctrine card

## Use this card for
Studying the **publicly observable AI-installation / fractional-AI-department model** as a
competitive pattern. Reach for this card when building a similar offer and the question is how to
**position** an AI-installation offer (price against headcount, not software), how to **package** the
whole offer into a single legible artifact, what shape the **content flywheel and funnel** should take,
and how a one-founder AI-services business can **evolve into two stacked products** over time.

## Core doctrine (observed competitive pattern)
Extracted as reusable patterns, not as a fixed menu to copy.

- **(1) "Fractional AI Department" framing — anchor price against payroll/headcount, not software.**
  The dominant observed move is to frame the offer as an *analogue to a fractional executive hire*: a
  bundle of AI "roles" that stands in for a department, sold on the logic of replacing salary line
  items. Price is anchored against what those roles cost as humans (a CSM, a CFO, a media buyer), so
  the buyer compares the offer to **payroll**, not to a $2–3K/mo automation tool. This is the central
  positioning lever: it lifts the price ceiling out of the "software/automation" category entirely.
  The reusable pattern is *category re-anchoring* — choose the comparison set that makes your price
  look small. Invent your own brand name for the mechanism; do not borrow his.

- **(2) The org-chart-as-offer artifact — compress the whole offer into one picture.**
  The offer is made legible by collapsing it into a **single AI "org chart"**: a set of named agents,
  each mapped to a recognizable business role / C-suite function (sales, onboarding, client success,
  content, ops/finance, an internal assistant), each with its own responsibility doc and knowledge
  base. The artifact does the selling — it converts a vague "we install AI" into something a 7–8 figure
  owner can *see* and map onto their existing team. The reusable pattern is **one visual that makes an
  abstract service concrete and mappable to roles the buyer already pays for**. Treat the specific
  role list as an *example*, not a fixed menu — your chart should reflect your own install, not be
  copied agent-for-agent.

- **(3) "Replace roles, not workflows" positioning.**
  The differentiation observed is selling **outcomes against headcount** ("install an AI workforce that
  replaces repetitive roles"), explicitly distancing from the crowded "I'll build you some n8n/Make
  automations for a monthly fee" market. Vocabulary is disciplined and consistent: *workforce,
  department, roles, payroll savings* — never *workflow, automation, bot*. The deeper, more honest
  version visible in his own long-form is softer than the headline: he says AI **does not fully replace
  humans yet** — it removes *SOP-able* roles (media buying, tech/onboarding, anything purely
  procedural) while humans (closers, CSMs) stay, and the team shrinks to a few high-leverage,
  *more-expensive* specialists (an AI/innovation lead + a prompt engineer). The reusable pattern:
  **lead with the role-replacement frame for positioning, but scope the actual promise to SOP-able
  work** — over-claiming full headcount replacement is where this gets dishonest.

- **(4) Content flywheel + funnel shape — live-build content → survey-as-lead-magnet → call.**
  The observed funnel: **YouTube long-form "live build" videos** (screen-share builds of real, named
  businesses; archetype = *timer + dollar figure + named vertical*, e.g. "automated an $X/yr business
  in N minutes") drive discovery → a **tracked link** → a short, ~4-question **"AI ROI Audit"
  survey** that doubles as the lead magnet (instant personalized projection, cheap to build, hard to
  copy) → results → **sales call** (internally framed as an *audit call*, which maps 1:1 to a paid
  diagnostic booking step). He reportedly runs a **Cole-Gordon-style VSL** on his offer and
  mastermind pages —
  see `cole-gordon.md` for that template. The reusable patterns: **(a) demonstration-as-marketing** (show
  the build, don't describe it), **(b) survey-as-lead-magnet**, **(c) an audit-framed booking step**.
  Caveat from the research: this is a *grind* engine, not a viral one — it compounds slowly over many
  months on a high, disciplined cadence; the model captures through the funnel, not through reach.

- **(5) Two-product evolution — DFY for businesses + a license/mastermind for agency owners.**
  The observed model evolves into **two stacked offers under one founder**: a Done-For-You AI install
  for end businesses (credibility loop + cash) *and* a higher-margin "license the system / mastermind"
  product sold to **agency owners** who want to install the same playbook themselves. The DFY proves
  it works; the license/mastermind is the margin compounder. **Important caveat:** the second product
  only works once there is a **real, demonstrable multi-year operating track record** behind it
  ("license what scaled *my* agency"). The reusable pattern is *earn the right to license by operating
  first* — model the license track from day one, but do not launch it before the receipts exist.

## Decision rules
Converted from the competitive research notes' steal/avoid lists into advisor-style rules.

- **Lead with a productized mechanism brand, not a personal brand.** His funnel rests on a personal
  origin story ($3M agency, ad-spend receipts) most people entering this market do not have. Until
  comparable proof exists, position the *named mechanism / offer brand* out front and let founder
  identity emerge behind it.
- **Anchor price against payroll, name the mechanism yourself.** Adopt the *category re-anchoring*
  (headcount, not software) and the disciplined role-vocabulary — but invent your own brand name and
  never reuse his headline, copy, or guarantee.
- **Build the one-picture offer artifact before launch.** Make your own org-chart-style visual that
  maps the install to roles the buyer already pays for. The artifact carries the pitch.
- **Scope the promise to SOP-able work; don't over-claim full replacement.** Sell role-replacement as
  the frame, but promise what the install actually does (remove procedural roles, augment the rest).
- **Build the survey-as-lead-magnet + audit-framed booking step early — it's cheap and defensible.**
- **Run cold outbound + referral in parallel; do not bet the business on slow-compounding content.**
  His whole engine is YouTube live-builds that take 6–18 months to compound; the data shows a flat,
  grind channel. Treat content as a long-term asset, not the acquisition plan. (X / under-built channels
  are where he is weakest — a gap, not a model to copy.)
- **Don't build proprietary infra (custom attribution / agent wrappers) early.** That's large dev cost
  before volume justifies it. Ride existing tooling until scale demands otherwise.
- **Don't copy the "we serve 7–9 figure businesses" anchor.** His real case studies start lower than
  the aspirational ICP he advertises. Pick a narrower, honest ICP.
- **Park the license/mastermind until there's a real operating track record.** Build DFY first; let the
  license track follow the receipts, not precede them.
- **Watch the drift, don't copy it.** The research shows his packaging visibly tilting toward
  agency-scaling / personal-income content even though his AI-implementation content performs better —
  i.e. he is diluting his own ICP. Keep 100% of your packaging pointed at the ICP you chose.
