## Core doctrine
- **Pick a market with a tailwind.** A rising, attention-drawing market (AI right now) forgives
  mistakes a flat market punishes — momentum carries a mediocre execution further than skill carries
  a great one in a dead category. Filter an opportunity on three things at once: the value is
  objectively provable, it can be sold to *cold* traffic without heavy emotional selling, and the
  market is already expanding. If the buyer isn't already trying to solve the problem and the market
  isn't growing, expect acquisition to be hard — that's the signal to reconsider the market, not to
  push harder on the channel.
- **Systems and owned assets over tools.** The durable advantage isn't which automation tool you use;
  it's the repeatable system around it — qualification, booking, CRM routing, payment, onboarding,
  fulfillment, reporting — plus assets you own (audience, proof, distribution). Tools commoditize;
  systems and assets compound. Set goals *as systems*, not as outcomes ("post weekly," not "get
  famous").
- **Backend architecture before front-end polish.** Build the plumbing that makes the promise
  deliverable and repeatable *before* over-investing in brand, funnel polish, or design. Validate the
  promise manually first (get one buyer to pay for an early version), then systemize the backend, then
  beautify. Polishing the storefront before the supply chain works is wasted motion.
- **AI is leverage for a small team, not a replacement for judgment.** Use AI to remove linear
  hiring — automate research, audit, documentation, reporting, QA, and repetitive build/support steps
  first. Keep human judgment on sales, diagnosis, and sensitive client communication until the pattern
  is proven. The win is capacity per person, not a fully autonomous machine.

## AI-agency field notes (DOCTRINE-LOW)
Field observation from operators running AI-automation shops (Ottley / Herk / Johnny). Treat as
texture, not as rules — single-operator, self-promotional sources.
- **What drove growth, per them:** building in public / content as the lead engine (small channels
  still pull inbound because algorithms target intent); reps over theory (you learn "how AI helps a
  business" only by doing the first 5–10 messy projects); going *non-technical → technical by brute
  force* (read JSON, learn ~10 core nodes, lean on LLMs for code) rather than learning to code.
- **Reported failure modes:** chasing every new AI release instead of doubling down on what's already
  working; selling generic "AI tools" or no-code demos that need real code in production; the
  "productized agent sold infinitely" dream (every business's SOP differs, so most builds stay
  custom); taking the big shiny $20–50k deal too early (it eats months, often falls through, leaves
  you with no experience); over-stuffing offers/communities with content (overwhelm is the top churn
  driver — structure beats volume).
- **Why the model has legs, per them:** still genuinely early (most buyers don't yet understand AI
  agents → long, education-heavy sales cycles, but a real moat); high retention and LTV once you hold
  the keys to a client's systems; "vibe automation" (text-to-workflow) is collapsing build time, which
  shifts the value from *building* toward *strategy / picking the right shots* and ongoing optimization.
- **Doctrine-relevant takeaways** (only the durable, niche-agnostic bits): cold traffic and content
  both work but content compounds; do the reps before chasing prestige work; less-but-structured beats
  more; the value migrates upstream to diagnosis/strategy as the building commoditizes. These echo the
  core cards (Hormozi constraint/starving-crowd, Cole cold-traffic truth test) — defer to those for
  any real decision.
