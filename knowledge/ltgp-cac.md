# LTGP/CAC — the cash-flow model (distilled)

> Primary source: Hormozi, "How I Would Build a Business in 2026 (If I Had to Start Over)"
> (YouTube `i7bLRKwKSms`). This is the single most load-bearing economics doctrine in the card.

## The one idea
The durable advantage in any business is **not a tactic, funnel, channel, or growth hack** — it's
the **model**: how much gross profit a customer creates versus what it costs to acquire them, and
how fast that cash returns. **Methods expire; models endure.** The number-one rule of business is
*don't run out of cash*, so the model that throws off cash fastest wins — it can outspend every
competitor in the attention auction and legally monopolize attention in its market.

The strategic test that beats every channel question:
> "Can we afford to *buy* customers (and outspend competitors)?" — not "can we get cheaper leads?"

## The formula
```
LTGP / CAC  =  Lifetime Gross Profit per Customer  /  Customer Acquisition Cost
```
- **LTGP** = cash kept after *delivery* costs, across the customer's lifetime. (Revenue − cost to deliver. If a $100 sale costs $20 to deliver, LTGP per purchase is $80; ten purchases = $800.) Sometimes called LTV / CLV.
- **CAC** = everything spent to *get* the customer — ads + marketing labor/contractors + content cost + sales commissions + any direct acquisition cost. (Note: cost to *acquire*, not cost to *deliver*.)

## Back-of-napkin calculation (use a full trailing year — it averages out good/bad months)
- **CAC** = total annual acquisition spend ÷ new customers. (e.g. $100,000 / 100 = $1,000.) Easiest number to get.
- **Lifetime revenue per customer** = total revenue ÷ total customers.
- **Cost per customer (delivery)** = total cost of delivery (COGS/fulfillment) ÷ total customers.
- **LTGP** = lifetime revenue per customer − cost per customer (or lifetime revenue × gross margin %).
- **Ratio** = LTGP ÷ CAC.

This is third-grade math (division + subtraction). If you can't run it, you don't understand your
business well enough — that's the gap to close, not an excuse to skip it.

## Automation-adjusted minimum ratios (the crux)
The "3:1" rule the software world quotes is only the floor, and only when the business is fully
automated. The minimum ratio rises as more of the business depends on **manual human labor**. The
three components that matter: **lead generation, conversion (sales), and delivery (fulfillment).**

| Automated components (of 3) | Minimum LTGP/CAC |
|---|---|
| All 3 automated (lead gen + conversion + fulfillment) | **3:1** |
| 2 of 3 automated | **6:1** |
| 1 of 3 automated | **9:1** |
| 0 — humans in every step | **12:1+** |

What counts as "automated / high-leverage": one-to-many lead gen (content, paid ads), automated
sales/checkout, and software/systematized delivery. What counts as "manual": one-to-one outreach,
human-closed sales, hands-on per-client delivery. Each manual component forces the required ratio up.

**Why the ratio must rise with manual labor** (the cushion argument):
- CAC always rises over time — colder markets, higher CPMs, more competitors. Today's cost per customer is likely the best you'll ever get.
- Scaling adds management layers and infrastructure cost.
- Later customers are less pre-sold and often worth less.
- Every new hire (a 6th salesperson, a new marketer, a new delivery rep) ramps below the team for months while costing from day one — so metrics dip exactly when you scale, and you need padding to stay cash-positive through it.

## The 30-day gross-profit payback rule
Even more practical than the ratio for a bootstrapped operator: **how fast does the acquisition
cash come back?** Limit the target window to ~30 days, because that's about as long as a small
business can float cash and it matches the interest-free window on credit. The client-financed
loop: put cash in the account, run ads, collect gross profit within 30 days that exceeds CAC,
roll it back in tomorrow — the customers finance the growth. **LTV cannot save a business that
runs out of cash before payback.**

## Ways to raise LTGP
- Raise the price.
- Decrease delivery cost (economies of scale, better terms, automation/AI support).
- Add **upsells** (more / higher quality of the same thing).
- Add **cross-sells** (a different complementary thing — fries with the burger, not a bigger burger).
- Add **downsells** *carefully* — capture non-buyers without cannibalizing premium buyers.
- Add **financing / payment terms** to pull cash forward.
- **Front-load payment** before fulfillment where appropriate ("we don't start until paid in full").

(Test: you should be able to apply all of these to *any* product on the spot — a book, a table, a
service. If you can't, you don't understand the business well enough.)

## Ways to lower CAC
- Improve the **offer** (more compelling = cheaper to acquire).
- Improve **ad creative** — volume + quality of hooks, remake your winners.
- Improve **CRO** — split-test pages, follow-up, phone scripts; train the sales team.
- Test **cheaper CPM channels** — but only if customer *quality* and LTGP hold.

## Why the model beats channel tactics (decision rules)
- **Don't optimize for cheap leads or cheap CAC in isolation. Optimize for return per acquisition dollar.** $500 CAC → $5,000 LTGP (10:1) beats $200 CAC → $1,000 LTGP (5:1), even though the customer costs more than twice as much.
- If acquisition feels expensive, **inspect the model before blaming the channel** — price, offer, gross margin, terms, upsells/cross-sells, payback, delivery cost. "Facebook doesn't work" is usually a model problem, not a channel problem.
- Wanting cheaper leads means competing on **methods** — copyable and expiring. A competitor with a better **model** outspends you anyway. The model is the moat.
- If you need a strict fixed marketing budget because every new customer starts cash-negative, **fix the model/payback before scaling spend.**
- If scaling depends on a founder personally selling or delivering, **require a higher ratio** — manual labor creates capacity shocks that demand cash cushion.
- Better model > better method. Cheaper leads are fine *today*; a better model compounds *forever*.
