# Cole Gordon — doctrine card

## Use this card for
High-ticket **offer construction**: how to package, position, name the mechanism, stack proof, set
appointment/sales economics, and read market sophistication to know which lever the offer must pull.
Reach for Cole when the question is "is this offer differentiated enough to survive cold traffic, and
will it command premium price?" — offer shape, positioning, proof density, guarantee *structure*, and
the two-call / appointment-economics motion.

**Defer to Hormozi when:** the question is the pure value equation, value-based pricing math, lead
generation (Core Four / starving crowd), money-model/payback design, or scaling constraints. Hormozi
frames the general strategy and the economics; Cole tunes the *packaging and positioning* of a
high-ticket offer on top of it. The two overlap heavily on offers — when both are loaded, Cole owns
specificity/mechanism/proof; Hormozi owns value-equation and downside-protected guarantee design.

## Core doctrine (distilled, evergreen)

- **Offer is the root lever (~80% of the outcome).** Cole's claim from studying 3,000+ businesses:
  the offer — not the funnel, team, or work ethic — decides most of the result. The offer sets
  pricing power, the cost curve (margin scalability), whether ads convert, how well the sales team
  closes, and ultimately LTV:CAC, which is the runway you have to scale. Mediocre operators win on a
  great offer; great operators stall on a weak one. **Fix the offer first.**

- **Packaging / Economics / Scalability triad.** A strong offer needs all three:
  *packaging* (differentiated positioning that makes a cold prospect care),
  *economics* (enough market power to charge premium and hold margin),
  *scalability* (a delivery cost curve that does not blow up per new client).
  Failure modes: packaging+scalability without economics → thin margins, no hiring power;
  packaging+economics without scalability → fulfillment bottleneck; economics+scalability without
  packaging → cold acquisition simply won't fire.

- **Cold traffic is the truth test.** Referrals and content carry a trust layer that masks weak
  positioning; cold outbound/ads have no trust layer, so they expose a weak offer immediately. If
  cold acquisition is slow, suspect the offer's specificity and proof before blaming the channel.

- **The 3S formula.** Communicate the offer as a **specific problem**, for a **specific person**, in a
  **specific way**. The "specific way" is the unique mechanism — and Cole's sharpening of it: the
  mechanism must *simultaneously explain why everything the prospect tried before failed and why this
  is different*. The anti-pattern is "full-service everything for everybody" (no specific person, no
  specific problem, no unique methodology → invisible).

- **Market power = price elasticity.** A differentiated offer holds conversion as price rises; a
  commodity offer loses conversion the moment price moves. Premium positioning generates the cash to
  out-acquire, out-hire, and out-deliver competitors. If price feels capped, the fix is tighter
  positioning + proof + a sharper mechanism, not a discount.

- **Barbell positioning — avoid no-man's-land.** Two viable ends of the barbell:
  (a) a highly leveraged / done-with-you product to a broad mass market, or
  (b) a more done-for-you / premium product to a smaller, richer market.
  No-man's-land is the middle: a small TAM of clients too poor to pay premium and too unestablished
  for done-with-you. Pick an end on purpose.

- **Target wealthy-but-below-average.** Cole splits buyer problems into *pain* (moving from below
  average back to average — urgent) and *unfulfilled desire* (moving from average to excellence —
  weaker pull). The best segment combines urgency with ability to pay: buyers who can afford the
  solution and feel below average and urgently want to get back to average (pain), not buyers chasing
  marginal excellence.

- **You channel existing desire, you don't create it.** Desire already exists in the market; you
  cannot manufacture it. You either place the offer in front of existing desire, or use messaging to
  bridge an existing desire into the thing you actually sell. Existing desires to channel: more
  customers/jobs, staff who solve problems, revenue/profit, risk reduction, status, health,
  relationships, time freedom.

- **Proof stacking.** Dense proof materially lifts cold response and book rates. Proof should come
  from *similar* customers and be stacked aggressively wherever compliant (ads, outbound, sales
  assets). Volume of named, niche-specific proof beats a single polished testimonial.

- **Done-for-you "cheese in broccoli" hybrids.** Add a partial done-for-you component to make the
  offer tangible and more attractive *without* taking on full ongoing fulfillment (which would wreck
  scalability). The broccoli is the deeper thing they need; the cheese is the done-for-you hook that
  gets them in. Done-for-you offers can also "get away with" a stronger guarantee because perceived
  value is higher.

- **Conditional guarantees only (for high-ticket).** Guarantees mainly help marketing
  differentiation. For high-ticket, every guarantee should be **conditional** — and the conditions
  should be exactly the success actions the client has to do anyway (access provided, inputs
  delivered, response standards met, no off-system handling). That makes the guarantee a qualifier,
  not an abuse vector. Cole himself rates the open-ended "we work with you until you get the result"
  variant as *weak* — "better than nothing," not a strong offer.

### Market sophistication (Eugene Schwartz, 5 stages) — which lever the offer must pull
Cole builds the offer **for the stage of the market, not for the product**. This is grounded — he
names Schwartz / *Breakthrough Advertising* directly.

| Stage | State of market | Winning lever |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First to market / naive | Bold, direct claim ("automate your company with AI") |
| 2 | Competitors arrive | Bigger / better / inflated claim ("…guaranteed") |
| 3 | Overloaded, distrustful | **Unique mechanism** (named, specific) |
| 4 | Sophisticated, jaded | **Identity + proof density + risk reversal + experience** |
| 5 | Hyper-saturated | Subtle insider language, deep belonging |

The further along the stage, the more the offer must lead with *how it works* (mechanism) and then
*who you become / who vouches* (identity + proof), because direct claims have stopped landing.

- **One mechanism, named as a proper noun.** A winning offer carries exactly one unique mechanism,
  injected across a small set of steps (the reconstruction says four; keep it few). More than one
  mechanism = complexity = death. The mechanism needs a trademark-style proprietary name — that name
  is what owns mindshare in an overloaded market. [INFERRED]
- **Two mechanism recipes:** *Parallel Solution Stacking* (first in the niche to combine roles/stack,
  or to wire in a disruptive tech) and *Minimalist Mechanism* (differentiation by radical simplicity —
  "9-word reactivation email," "one-click bookkeeping"). Pick one. [INFERRED]
- **Each step named after the benefit, not the activity**, and engineered to be simple, unexpected,
  credible (borrowed authority/science, not just testimonials), and concrete (dated dollar outcomes,
  not "more sales"). [INFERRED]
- **Anti-positioning:** name the dominant prior approach and explain why it's broken *now* — this is
  the Stage-3/4 move that makes the mechanism legible. [INFERRED]

## Decision rules
- If ads/outbound are slow → inspect offer specificity (3S) and proof density before assuming the
  channel is broken.
- If pricing feels capped → raise market power with tighter positioning, more proof, and a sharper
  named mechanism; don't discount.
- If fulfillment is breaking → redesign the promise/delivery so the cost curve scales (add partial
  done-for-you, not full done-for-you).
- If prospects "don't want what you sell" → bridge it to an existing desire; never try to educate
  demand into existence.
- If you're stuck in the middle of the barbell → move deliberately to one end (leveraged-to-broad or
  done-for-you-to-rich).
- When choosing a segment → favor *pain* (below-average → average) with money over *unfulfilled
  desire* (average → excellence).
- When designing a guarantee → make it conditional, and set the conditions to the exact behaviors that
  make success likely; never open-ended.
- Diagnose the market's sophistication stage first → it dictates whether you lead with a claim, a
  mechanism, or identity+proof.

## Conflicts with other advisors
- **Guarantee aggressiveness — Cole vs Hormozi.** Cole leans into bold outcome/risk-reversal to win
  cold trust and differentiate; Hormozi designs the guarantee to survive bad-fit buyers and protect
  the downside. The real trade-off: a stronger guarantee lifts conversion but invites abuse. **Default:**
  the user's live guarantee wins; conditional/milestone/scoped-credit/ownership structures only —
  never "work free until outcome," which creates uncapped delivery liability for a service business.
  (See `cross-advisor/conflict-map.md`, Guarantee row, and `cross-advisor/guarantee-rules.md`.)
- **Offers split with Hormozi.** When both cards are open on an offer question, Cole owns the
  *packaging/positioning* layer (3S specificity, mechanism naming, proof density, sophistication
  stage); Hormozi owns the *value-equation and pricing math* layer. State both, don't average them.
